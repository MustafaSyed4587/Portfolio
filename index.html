Project 01 · Data analysis

TTC Bus Delay Analysis

Python · pandas · Data visualization · Public data

Diversions are 8% of TTC bus delay incidents but 40% of delay minutes. The typical diversion lasts 70 minutes, against 12 for everything else.

  • Cleaned 102,257 City of Toronto records, removing 298 duplicates and 12,962 zero-minute entries to isolate 88,997 delay incidents.
  • Analyzed 46 delay codes grouped into 7 causes, then compared incidents, minutes, and medians by cause, hour, weekday, and route.
  • Recommended reporting diversions separately, replacing the vague "Other" equipment code (20% of incidents), and adding spare-operator cover for the 2 to 7 pm rush.

Open the dashboard

Built on the City of Toronto's open TTC Bus Delay Data. Not affiliated with the TTC.

40%of delay minutes come from diversions

Delay incidents by hour of day

Afternoon rush, 2 pm to 7 pm, highlighted. Hover or tab to a bar for the count.

How I built it
The question

Where do TTC bus delay minutes come from, and what could be done about them?

The method

Cleaned the raw City of Toronto file in Python and pandas, grouped 46 delay codes into 7 causes, and compared incidents, total minutes, and medians by cause, hour, weekday, and route.

The hard part

The raw file had duplicates and zero-minute rows that would have skewed every average. I removed them and kept a count of each removal so the final numbers can be checked.

What I would do next

Add route-level trip counts so delay can be measured per trip, and test whether the 2 to 7 pm pattern holds in every season.

Project 02 · Security and privacy

Case study · hypothetical organization

Privacy and Security Risk Assessment

Risk analysis · PIPEDA · Cybersecurity basics

For an invented volunteer-run charity, two risks rate High and eight Medium. Four fixes in the first week remove most of the exposure.

  • Mapped the personal data the charity holds and ranked it by sensitivity.
  • Scored 10 risks on likelihood and impact, and reviewed its practices against the 10 principles of Canada's PIPEDA privacy law.
  • Planned a 30-day fix list ordered by risk removed per hour, starting with two-step sign-in and access clean-up.

How I built it
The question

How exposed is a small volunteer-run charity that holds personal information, and what should it fix first?

The method

Mapped the data it holds, scored 10 risks on likelihood and impact, and rated its practices against the 10 principles of PIPEDA, checking each legal point against the Privacy Commissioner of Canada.

The hard part

A risk score says how bad something is, not what to do first. I added a second ranking, risk removed per hour of effort, to separate quick wins from long projects.

What I would do next

Project 03 · Data and databases

Practice project · generated data

Donation Drive Database and Analysis

SQL · SQLite · Database design · Python

In this practice database, school drives collect 3.2 lbs of food per volunteer hour. Community centres collect 8.2.

  • Designed four linked tables (drives, donations, shifts, distributions) with primary keys, foreign keys, and a rule that rejects zero or negative weights.
  • Queried it with joins, grouping, and subqueries to compare donation categories, sites, and months.
  • Found that canned goods are 40% of all pounds, and that school drives are the outlier, at less than half the pounds per volunteer hour of any other site.

All data is randomly generated to practice the method. It is not the records of any real organization.

4linked tables, 22 drives, 215 donation records
2.6xvolunteer efficiency, best site against schools
40%of pounds are canned goods

Ask the database

Pick a question to see what it returns, and the query I wrote to answer it.


      
How I built it

        
The question
Can a few linked tables answer which drive sites use volunteer time best?

        
The method
Designed four tables with keys and a rule against invalid weights, loaded generated data in SQLite, and wrote joins and grouped queries to compare categories, sites, and months.

        
A trap I avoided
Joining donations straight to shifts would have counted each shift several times. I summed donations per drive first, then joined, so totals stayed correct.

        
What I would do next
Run it on a real, consented dataset, add indexes, and build a simple dashboard on top of the queries.

How I work
Four habits I use on every project

    

      
1
Start with the question
I ask what decision the work is for before I open any data, so the analysis answers something real.
TTC: where do delay minutes come from?

      
2
Clean, and show my cleaning
I count what I removed and why, so anyone can check the numbers I end up with.
TTC: 298 duplicates, 12,962 zero-minute rows

      
3
Find the number that changes the answer
I look for the comparison that makes the point, and use medians when a few extremes distort the average.
8% of incidents, 40% of minutes

      
4
Explain it, and say what it can't show
I write for the person who has to act, and I state the limits of the data up front.
Incidents only, so no delay per trip

    

  


  

    
Field notes
Things my projects taught me

    

      
Use the median when a few extremes shout
The typical TTC diversion lasted 70 minutes, against 12 for everything else. A plain average would have blurred that gap and hidden where the delay really sits.

      
Count what you throw away
Removing 298 duplicates and 12,962 zero-minute rows was the right call, but only because I wrote the numbers down. Cleaning you cannot explain is cleaning nobody can trust.

      
Vague categories hide problems
One catch-all equipment code made up 20% of TTC incidents. When a fifth of your data says "other," the first fix is better labels, not more analysis.
